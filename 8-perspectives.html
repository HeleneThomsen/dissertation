7  Future perspectives

The findings of this thesis do not currently support direct changes to clinical practice but identify several directions for future research.

7.1 CGM in future Cardiovascular Risk Prediction

Foundation models trained on CGM data may eventually enable more comprehensive use of glucose dynamics for cardiovascular risk assessment than traditional CGM-derived metrics. As machine learning methods continue to advance, alongside rapid developments in continuous glucose monitoring and automated insulin delivery technologies, the potential clinical value of CGM-derived information is likely to increase. This is particularly relevant given the increasing global prevalence of diabetes and its associated cardiovascular burden.

Although traditional CGM-derived metrics provided some predictive value for elevated NT-proBNP, age alone performed similarly while being substantially easier, less expensive, and non-invasive to obtain. Consequently, the additional clinical value of CGM-derived information must outweigh the increased burden and cost associated with collecting CGM data. However, for individuals who already use CGM as part of routine diabetes management, glucose-derived information may provide complementary insights into cardiovascular risk without requiring additional data collection. Unlike age, CGM-derived metrics reflect an individual’s temporal glucose variability, which may capture aspects of metabolic health that are potentially modifiable.

7.2 Future Research on CGM Data and Prediction of Cardiovascular Outcomes

The potential relevance of CGM data for cardiovascular risk assessment is increasingly recognized, as reflected by the ongoing longitudinal FACULTY study, which includes approximately 70,000 participants using CGM devices.1 This study may provide an important opportunity to determine whether CGM-derived information adds prognostic value beyond established clinical predictors. However, studies of this scale are not always feasible, and progress in the field will also depend on the ability to synthesize evidence across smaller studies. In this regard, the heterogeneity identified in Study I highlights the need for greater methodological consistency. Wider adherence to consensus recommendations,2 could promote more consistent calculation and reporting of CGM-derived metrics. This should be accompanied by prespecified approaches to covariate adjustment, harmonized definitions of cardiovascular outcomes, and transparent documentation of analytical decisions. Greater consistency across these areas would facilitate meaningful comparisons between studies and provide a stronger basis for future systematic reviews and meta-analyzes.

In addition to methodological alignment, greater use of routinely collected CGM data could support larger and more cost-efficient studies that more closely reflect populations currently using CGM devices. Study I demonstrated that such data remain underused in research on cardiovascular complications. This may partly reflect restricted access to proprietary manufacturer platforms and difficulties linking CGM measurements with individual-level clinical records. In other fields, including medical image analyzis, progress has been accelerated by large, publicly available datasets.3–5 However, no equivalent resource currently combines CGM time series with information on diabetes-related complications although efforts have been made to gather an overview of publicly available datasets containing CGM data.6–8 Developing shared research resources or making existing databases more findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable in accordance with the FAIR principles could support reproducible research and accelerate progress in this field.9

Greater availability of large, well-characterized datasets would also make it more feasible to train and fine-tune CGM foundation models for cardiovascular applications. In particular, multimodal models could integrate CGM time series with established cardiovascular risk factors, laboratory measurements, treatment information, and data from other wearable devices. This may allow models to capture complementary information across data sources rather than relying solely on predefined CGM-derived metrics. Shared datasets would further enable standardized benchmarking and independent external evaluation of such models. However, future studies should determine whether the additional complexity provides clinically meaningful improvements beyond simpler and more interpretable approaches. Openly available models and reusable glucose representations could further reduce the resources required to investigate these approaches and enable researchers to adapt pretrained models to smaller, task-specific datasets. Nevertheless, open access alone does not ensure generalizability, particularly if the underlying training data do not adequately represent the populations in which the models are intended to be used in.

7.3 Fairness in Future Clinical Risk Prediction

Future efforts to develop and implement models for cardiovascular risk prediction should consider the diversity of the intended target population from the beginning. Adequate representation of racial, ethnic, and other minority groups is important not only during model development and external validation but also when evaluating clinical impact. A model exhibiting performance disparities across groups may reinforce existing healthcare inequities, particularly when underrepresentation in the training data reflects unequal access to care or health technologies. In such cases, the model may perform least effectively for groups that are already underserved, further limiting their opportunity to benefit from its implementation. Fairness assessments should therefore consider not only whether predictive performance differs across groups but also whether all groups can access and benefit from the intervention and whether its implementation reduces or reinforces existing healthcare inequities. These considerations should be guided by the characteristics and healthcare context of the intended population and revisited throughout model development, external evaluation, implementation, and post-deployment monitoring.
