5  Discussion

The overall aim of this thesis was to investigate the potential of CGM data and machine learning methods for cardiovascular risk assessment. To address this aim, the thesis combined a systematic evaluation of the existing literature with the assessment of foundation model-derived glucose representations and an investigation of algorithmic fairness in glucose prediction. The following discussion integrates the findings of these three studies, and considers their methods and results in relation to existing literature.

5.1 Evidence for CGM in Cardiovascular Risk Assessment

Through the literature 53 studies were identified as investigating the link between CGM-data and CVD outcomes. There were very inconsistent findings across the literature. A wide range of CGM-derived metrics were investigated in relation to both clinical and subclinical cardiovascular outcomes, and the statistical adjustment strategies varied considerably between studies. It was observed that studies focusing on people with T2D had bigger study populations and more consistently found evidence for associations compared to studies focusing on people with T1D which may indicate a lack of statistical power in the studies with T1D, which may explain some of the inconsistencies across the studies uncovered in the scoping review. The current evidence base is also limited by its geographical concentration mainly in Asia and Europe and by its almost exclusive focus on association analyses. Only three studies included some prediction done with MAGE which shows a knowledge gap in the literature.

The most frequently investigated CGM-derived metric was TIR, especially in analysis with clinical CVD however inconsistent results were found across studies and similar results were found in1 The second most frequently investigated CGM-derived metric was MAGE. It seemed more consistently associated with coronary artery disease and severity scores, despite the smaller sample size compared to TIR however this is only based on a small section of the included studies. Furthermore, MAGE was the only CGM-derived metric evaluated for its predictive performance, and this was limited to coronary artery disease and subclinical peripheral artery disease. Although MAGE outperformed HbA1c as a predictor in these studies,2,Mi2012? the prediction models demonstrated only modest discriminative ability. This may indicate that within-day glucose variability is a relevant cardiovascular risk factor. However, MAGE is not a complete measure of glucose variability and is biased toward detecting hyperglycemic excursions.ScR57? Consequently, MAGE may underestimate the contribution of hypoglycemia to cardiovascular risk. Whereas some studies have reported associations between hyperglycemia-focused CGM-derived metrics and cardiovascular outcomes,3,ScR43? others have reported associations between hypoglycemia-related CGM-derived metrics and cardiovascular outcomes,Magri?,ScR39?,ScR46?,ScR58?,ScR59?,ScR60? with some concluding that hypoglycemic excursions are associated with macrovascular complications, whereas hyperglycemic excursions are associated with microvascular complications.4,Mesa2022? Together, these findings suggest that although within-day glucose variability may be an important cardiovascular risk factor, both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia may contribute to cardiovascular risk and should be considered when evaluating CGM-derived metrics.

Another component playing into the heterogeneity of the studies where the difference in how the statistical analysis were performed. CGM-derived metrics were defined calculated over different time periods or split up into ranges and thresholds were changed for acr{TIR}, time below range (TBR) and time above range (TAR) across different studies. Adjustment strategies varied substantially between studies, reflecting differences in study objectives and analytical approaches. Although most studies adjusted for established cardiovascular risk factors, particularly demographic characteristics, medical history, biochemical variables, and anthropometric measures, the covariates varied between studies, limiting direct comparability of the reported associations Only a minority of studies adjusted for additional CGM-derived metrics. Whether such adjustment is appropriate depends on the research question, as many CGM-derived metrics capture overlapping aspects of glycemic control. Furthermore, no substantial differences were observed between unadjusted and the most extensively adjusted estimates, suggesting that adjustment for additional covariates generally had only a modest influence on the reported associations.

The heterogeneity of the literature, the limited evidence supporting individual CGM-derived metrics, and the challenges of describing glucose variability through CGM-derived metrics provided part of the rationale for evaluating foundation model-derived representations in the subsequent studies, as the complete CGM time series can be used directly without first reducing it to manually engineered CGM-derived metrics. As a result, information on hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia, mean glucose, glucose variability within and between days, and temporal glucose patterns can all be incorporated simultaneously.

5.2 Learning Meaningful Representations from CGM data

Study II showed that CGMformer, the foundation model developed by Lu et al.5, captured both long- and short-term glucose fluctuations, as assessed using conventional CGM-derived metrics, in individuals with T1D and T2D. However, the learned representations (embeddings) did not show clear patterns when stratified by CVD-related variables or race. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that CGMformer can generalize beyond its original training population of Chinese individuals with normal glucose tolerance, impaired glucose regulation, and T2D, because it captured clinically meaningful glycemic patterns in both the Danish CANCAN cohort of people with advanced T2D and the US T1DX cohort of people with T1D.

In our study, cosine distances were lower within-participants than between-participants, indicating that the CGM embeddings captured participant-specific patterns. Similar findings have been reported by Lu et al,5 who observed higher within-participant cosine similarity, and by Lutsker et al,6 who found lower within-participant cosine distances using the GLUFormer foundation model trained on an Israeli cohort from the Human Phenotype Project.7 Collectively, these findings support that CGM foundation models learn stable, individualized representations of glucose dynamics while also capturing common glycemic characteristics shared among individuals with similar glucose control.as well as generalize across independent populations and diabetes types. By learning directly from the complete CGM time series, these representations provide an alternative to manually engineered CGM-derived metrics and establish a reusable foundation for downstream clinical applications. Similar transitions from manually extracted metrics to learned representations have driven performance improvements in fields such as computer vision and natural language processing, where pretrained foundation models have become the standard approach for downstream prediction tasks.8 Whether these learned representations translate into improved predictive performance was subsequently investigated in Study II.

5.3 Incremental Value of CGM-derived Information for NT-proBNP Prediction

Although previous studies have successfully used foundation models to predict CVD-related outcomes, including macrovascular complications5 and cardiovascular mortality6, the learned representations in the present study did not improve prediction of elevated NT-proBNP beyond established clinical predictors and were associated with increased model complexity. The optimism-corrected performance estimates indicate that the more complex models were susceptible to overfitting, reinforcing the conclusion that the learned representations did not provide robust improvements beyond established clinical predictors. This does not necessarily indicate that foundation model-derived representations are unsuitable for cardiovascular prediction. Rather, their performance may depend on the downstream modeling strategy. In the present study, the embeddings were incorporated as fixed predictors in logistic regression models, whereas Lu et al.5 fine-tuned (transfer learning like transfer 1 in Figure 1.2) the CGMformer and achieved an AUROC above 0.70 for predicting macrovascular complications. Similarly, Lutsker et al.6 demonstrated that a GLUFormer-derived risk score outperformed HbA1c in stratifying individuals according to cardiovascular-related mortality risk. However, the limited sample size and the lack of datasets containing both CGM data and cardiovascular outcomes precluded the use of more complex downstream models and task-specific fine-tuning as performed in Lu et al,5 and Lutsker et al,.6 Furthermore, reducing the embeddings to UMAP dimensions may have resulted in the loss of predictive information, which could partly explain the limited performance of the prediction models. The different findings may also reflect differences in the predicted outcomes. Whereas previous studies predicted clinical cardiovascular events or mortality, the present study focused on elevated NT-proBNP, a biomarker that is affected not only by cardiac dysfunction but also by factors such as age, sex and renal function.9 Consequently, glucose-derived embeddings may have a weaker relationship with NT-proBNP than with overt cardiovascular outcomes although this remains speculative Together, these findings suggest that the successful application of foundation model representations to complex clinical outcomes may depend on the downstream modelling strategy and having access to substantially larger datasets.

While learned representations did not translate into improved prediction of elevated NT-proBNP, TIR and MAGE as standalone predictors did. Traditional CGM-derived metrics remain highly relevant for clinical prediction. Metrics such as TIR quantify well-established aspects of glycemic control, are readily understood by clinicians, and are already incorporated into routine diabetes management, particularly TIR. Their simplicity and established clinical interpretation make them useful reference measures when assessing whether more complex learned representations provide additional predictive information. Previous studies have likewise demonstrated prognostic value for MAGE in individuals with acute coronary syndrome10,11, and greater glucose variability has been associated with higher NT-proBNP concentrations in this population11. Participants with elevated NT-proBNP in the CANCAN cohort exhibited characteristics consistent with more advanced disease but also had lower BMI and more favorable glycemic profiles. Although these findings may appear counterintuitive, lower BMI has previously been associated with poorer outcomes in patients with established heart failure, while obesity is associated with lower circulating NT-proBNP concentrations12. Overall, these findings illustrate that the relationship between glycemic control, body composition, and cardiovascular risk is complex underscoring the importance of assessing CGM-derived measures within multivariable prediction models

In Study II, when adding TIR and MAGE to the clinical prediction model resulted in a modest improvement in AUROC compared with the model based on clinical variables alone. However, the performance of these models should also be considered relative to a simpler benchmark model such as based on age alone. The simpler baseline model showed substantial predictive ability, consistent with the well-established association between increasing age and NT-proBNP concentrations in both healthy populations and individuals with T2D.13 Together, these findings suggest that although readily available clinical variables capture much of the information relevant for predicting elevated NT-proBNP, CGM-data may still provide information that could contribute to future cardiovascular risk assessment.

5.4 Fairness of CGM-based Machine Learning Models

While Studies II and III focused on whether foundation model-derived representations and conventional CGM-derived metrics improved cardiovascular risk prediction, the final aim of the thesis considered another important aspect of machine learning models: whether their performance is equitable across population subgroups.

Although the primary focus of this thesis is cardiovascular risk assessment, glucose forecasting provides an appropriate setting for investigating algorithmic fairness in CGM-based machine learning. In contrast to participant-level cardiovascular prediction, glucose forecasting is trained on a continuous stream of CGM measurements, allowing each participant to contribute numerous prediction windows (samples) over the recording period rather than a single outcome. This results in substantially more training examples, enabling the development and evaluation of more complex machine learning models. Consequently, glucose forecasting provides a suitable framework for studying how training data composition influences model performance across demographic groups. Furthermore, automated insulin delivery (AID) systems are becoming increasingly sophisticated, with the long-term goal of approximating the physiological regulation of glucose by a healthy pancreas. However, the delayed onset and peak action of subcutaneously administered insulin limit the ability of these systems to respond rapidly to changing glucose concentrations.14 Consequently, accurate short-term glucose forecasting has become increasingly important, particularly for anticipating postprandial glucose excursions and enabling earlier insulin adjustments to reduce the risk of both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia.14 As prediction models become increasingly integrated into diabetes management, ensuring that they perform equitably across patient populations is essential.15

Study III demonstrated that varying the racial composition of the training dataset was associated with statistically significant differences in model performance across racial groups when predicting future blood glucose values. Although these differences were statistically significant, they were small and unlikely to result in clinically meaningful disparities. This interpretation was supported by the Surveillance Error Grid, which indicated that the observed differences in predictive performance between racial groups had limited clinical impact. Nevertheless, the findings suggest that training data composition should be considered when developing and evaluating machine learning models intended for clinical use. The observed performance differences appeared to disappear following transfer learning, further suggesting that including data from underrepresented groups may help reduce such differences.

Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of transfer learning to improve machine learning performance across a range of healthcare applications. For example, transfer learning has been associated with reduced performance disparities in multi-ethnic cancer omics datasets, suggesting that it may help mitigate the effects of imbalanced training data.Gao? Similar benefits have been reported for personalized glucose prediction, where transfer learning has improved model performance across several prediction tasks.Kushner?,Seo2021? In contrast, Mohebbi et al,Mohebbi? did not observe an improvement with transfer learning and speculated that the limited size of the fine-tuning dataset may have contributed to this finding. Overall, the present findings are largely consistent with the existing literature, as the transfer learned models achieved the best overall performance while also reducing the performance differences observed between racial groups.

The Surveillance Error Grid results were also comparable to those reported by van Doorn et al,16 who evaluated glucose prediction models in people with type 1 diabetes using a similar experimental setting. This agreement suggests that the clinical risk associated with prediction errors observed in the present study is consistent with previous evaluations of deep learning-based glucose forecasting models.

5.5 Methodological Considerations

The principal strength and value of the scoping review lie in its comprehensive mapping of the literature, including the identification of more relevant studies than any previous review on the topic, to the best of my knowledge, as well as its differentiation between association and prediction studies, identification of methodological heterogeneity, and characterization of important evidence gaps. However, the heterogeneity identified through this review also limited the feasibility of synthesizing effect estimates consistently across studies. The findings were therefore presented descriptively by mapping the number of studies investigating different CGM-derived metrics and cardiovascular outcomes. Although this approach provides an overview of the scope and distribution of the existing evidence, it does not allow definitive conclusions to be drawn about the strength or consistency of associations between specific CGM-derived metrics and cardiovascular outcomes.

Furthermore, the predominance of cross-sectional studies found in the scoping review further limited the clinical interpretation of the evidence identified. Without temporal separation between CGM exposure and cardiovascular outcomes, it is unclear whether the observed glucose patterns preceded cardiovascular disease or resulted from the disease itself, associated behavioural changes, or intensified treatment. Reverse causality could therefore either produce apparent associations that emerged only after the development of CVD or attenuate pre-existing associations through subsequent treatment and behavioural changes. Because few longitudinal studies were identified, it was not possible to determine whether this systematically influenced the findings or contributed to differences between cross-sectional and longitudinal studies.

Furthermore, the focus in the review was to ensure to include all relevant studies in what was deemed the two most relevant biomedical databases by having a broad search strategy, this might have led to missing relevant studies in other databases.

5.5.1 The CGMformer and Cosine Distances

By externally evaluating CGMformer, developed by Lu et al.,5 Study II contributes to the validation of existing models, as recommended by Efthimiou et al.17 and provides evidence regarding the foundation model’s generalizability. For the evaluation of the foundation model, by combining comparisons with established CGM-derived metrics, cosine-distance analyses, and visualizations, the study assessed whether the representations captured clinically interpretable glycemic patterns as well as participant-specific characteristics. This provided greater insight into the content and potential utility of the learned representations. A limitation of the cosine-distance analyses was the complex dependence structure of the observations. Participants contributed multiple days of CGM data and could therefore be represented in multiple distance comparisons. Consequently, the observations were not independent, making a conventional Mann–Whitney U test inappropriate. Statistical significance would also be difficult to interpret because the large number of pairwise comparisons does not represent an equally large number of independent observations. Moreover, treating all pairwise distances as independent would overstate the effective sample size and could produce misleadingly small p-values. The cosine-distance results were therefore evaluated descriptively and visually, enabling exploration of potential group differences but not formal statistical inference.

5.5.2 Prediction of NT-proBNP

The use of bootstrapping with optimism correction allowed for robust internal validation, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting and overestimating the results which are important factors to take into consideration when working with real-world limited data. The relatively small sample size restricted the analyses because the available data were deemed insufficient for task-specific fine-tuning of CGMformer, and the embeddings were instead incorporated as fixed predictors in comparatively simple downstream models. Reducing the embeddings to UMAP dimensions made the number of predictors manageable relative to the sample size but may have discarded information relevant to NT-proBNP. Consequently, the available data did not permit evaluation of the embeddings under task-specific fine-tuning, and their potential prognostic value therefore remains undetermined. Furthermore, dichotomizing NT-proBNP at 125 pg/mL may be an imperfect replacement for cardiovascular risk and heart failure, limiting the extent to which the analysis could assess the relationship between CGM-derived representations and clinical cardiovascular outcomes. A more definitive evaluation would require substantially larger longitudinal datasets containing temporally aligned CGM measurements, relevant clinical covariates, and subsequent cardiovascular outcomes. However, such datasets remain rare, limiting opportunities for task-specific fine-tuning or the development of more complex prediction models. Access to these data would enable assessment of whether the embeddings provide prognostic information beyond established cardiovascular risk factors and conventional CGM-derived metrics.

5.5.3 Fairness in Clinical Prediction Models

A key strength of study III was its systematic evaluation of how the racial composition of training data influences predictive performance across groups. By examining multiple training strategies and varying group representation in the training data, the study provides a framework that could also be applied to fairness evaluations involving other populations, outcomes, and data modalities. The use of openly available data, together with publicly shared analytical code, further enhances the transparency and reproducibility of the study and facilitates extension of the approach in future research. However, the study included only non-Hispanic White and Black participants from the United States. Future studies should evaluate potential disparities across additional racial and ethnic groups and in other geographical settings. Fairness evaluations could also consider other demographic characteristics, such as age, given that glycemic control varies across the lifespan.18 Although algorithmic fairness is most commonly evaluated for binary classification using established criteria such as demographic parity and equalized odds,19 comparable approaches for forecasting tasks remain less developed. Study III therefore provides a novel framework for evaluating disparities in glucose forecasting. However, the analysis was computationally intensive because models were repeatedly trained across multiple racial compositions and training strategies. Even though only a shallow neural network was evaluated, the computational requirements were substantial, which may limit the feasibility of applying this framework to more complex models without access to considerable computing resources. The findings may also depend on the selected 60-minute prediction horizon. Although this horizon was chosen to allow time for potential preventive action, shorter forecasting horizons generally yield greater predictive accuracy. Future studies should determine whether the observed effects of racial composition and transfer learning are consistent across prediction horizons and when using CGM data with higher temporal resolution. In addition, the framework was evaluated using only a single US dataset and was not replicated in an independent cohort. Because the forecasting models were trained separately for each participant, external validation of the fitted models themselves was not directly applicable. However, evaluation in an independent dataset would be necessary to determine whether the observed group-level disparities and the effects of training-data composition generalize to other populations, CGM devices, and healthcare settings.
