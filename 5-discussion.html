5  Discussion

The overall aim of this dissertation was to investigate the potential of CGM data and machine learning methods for cardiovascular risk assessment. To address this aim, the dissertation combined a systematic evaluation of the existing literature with the assessment of learned representations from a foundation model and an investigation of algorithmic fairness in glucose prediction. The following discussion integrates the findings of these three studies, and considers their methods and results in relation to existing literature.

5.1 Study I: Evidence for CGM in Cardiovascular Risk Assessment

Through the literature 53 studies investigating the link between CGM-data and CVD outcomes were identified. There were very inconsistent findings across the literature. A wide range of CGM-derived metrics were investigated in relation to both clinical and subclinical cardiovascular outcomes, and the statistical adjustment strategies varied considerably between studies. It was observed that studies focusing on people with T2D had bigger study populations and more consistently found evidence for associations compared to studies focusing on people with T1D which may indicate a lack of statistical power in the studies with T1D, which may explain some of the inconsistencies across the studies uncovered in the scoping review. The current evidence base is also limited by its geographical concentration mainly in Asia and Europe and by its almost exclusive focus on association analyzes. Only three studies evaluated the predictive value of MAGE, indicating that its potential for cardiovascular risk prediction remains insufficiently investigated.

5.1.1 TIR, MAGE and Cardiovascular Outcomes

TIR was the most frequently investigated CGM-derived metric, particularly for clinical CVD outcomes, although associations were inconsistent across studies, as also reported in a previous review.1 The second most frequently investigated CGM-derived metric was MAGE. It seemed more consistently associated with coronary artery disease and severity scores, despite the smaller sample size compared to TIR however this is strictly based on a small section of the included studies. Furthermore, MAGE was the only CGM-derived metric evaluated for its predictive performance, and this was limited to coronary artery disease and subclinical peripheral artery disease. Although MAGE outperformed HbA1c as a predictor in these studies,2,3 the prediction models demonstrated only modest discriminative ability. This may indicate that within-day glucose variability is a relevant cardiovascular risk factor. However, MAGE is not a complete measure of glucose variability and is biased toward detecting hyperglycemic excursions.4 Consequently, MAGE may underestimate the contribution of hypoglycemia to cardiovascular risk. However, the literature remains inconclusive regarding the relative importance of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia in relation to cardiovascular outcomes. Some studies have reported associations between hyperglycemia-focused CGM-derived metrics and cardiovascular outcomes,3,5 others have reported associations between hypoglycemia-related CGM-derived metrics and cardiovascular outcomes,6–11 with some concluding that hypoglycemic excursions are associated with macrovascular complications, whereas hyperglycemic excursions are associated with microvascular complications.6,10 Together, these findings suggest that although within-day glucose variability may be an important cardiovascular risk factor, both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia may contribute to cardiovascular risk and should be considered when evaluating CGM-derived metrics.

5.1.2 Study Designs

Another component contributing to the heterogeneity were the difference in how the statistical analyzes were performed across studies. CGM-derived metrics were defined over different time periods or split up into ranges and thresholds were changed for TIR, time below range (TBR) and time above range (TAR) across different studies. Adjustment strategies varied substantially between studies, reflecting differences in study objectives and analytical approaches. The covariates varied between studies even though most studies adjusted for established cardiovascular risk factors, particularly demographic characteristics, medical history, biochemical variables, and anthropometric measures, limiting direct comparability of the reported associations. Only a minority of studies adjusted for additional CGM-derived metrics. Whether such adjustment is appropriate depends on the research question, as many CGM-derived metrics capture overlapping aspects of glycemic control. Furthermore, no substantial differences were observed between unadjusted and the most extensively adjusted estimates, suggesting that adjustment for additional covariates generally had only a modest influence on the reported associations.

The heterogeneity of the literature and the challenges of describing glucose variability through CGM-derived metrics provided part of the rationale for evaluating foundation model-derived representations in the subsequent study, as the complete CGM time-series can be used directly without first reducing it to manually engineered CGM-derived metrics. As a result, information on hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia, mean glucose, glucose variability within and between days, and temporal glucose patterns can all be incorporated simultaneously.

5.2 Study II: Learning Meaningful Representations from CGM data

Study II showed that CGMformer, the foundation model developed by Lu et al.,12 captured both long- and short-term glucose fluctuations, as assessed using traditional CGM-derived metrics, in individuals with T1D and T2D. However, the learned representations (embeddings) did not show clear patterns when stratified by CVD-related variables or race. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that CGMformer can generalize beyond its original training population of Chinese individuals with normal glucose tolerance, impaired glucose regulation, and T2D, because it captured clinically meaningful glycemic patterns in both the Danish CANCAN cohort of people with advanced T2D and the US T1DX cohort of people with T1D.

In Study II, cosine distances were lower within-participants than between-participants, indicating that the CGM embeddings captured participant-specific patterns. Similar findings have been reported by Lu et al,12 who observed higher within-participant cosine similarity (1-cosine distance), and by Lutsker et al.,13 who found lower within-participant cosine distances using the GLUFormer foundation model trained on an Israeli cohort from the Human Phenotype Project.14 Collectively, these findings support that CGM foundation models learn stable, individualized representations of glucose dynamics while also capturing common glycemic characteristics shared among individuals with similar glucose control as well as generalize across independent populations and diabetes types. By learning directly from the complete CGM time-series, these representations provide an alternative to manually engineered CGM-derived metrics and establish a reusable foundation for downstream clinical applications. Similar transitions from manually extracted metrics to learned representations have driven performance improvements in fields such as computer vision and natural language processing, where pretrained foundation models have become the standard approach for downstream prediction tasks.15 Whether these learned representations translate into improved predictive performance was subsequently investigated in Study II.

5.3 Study II: Incremental Value of CGM-derived Information for NT-proBNP Prediction

Although previous studies12,13 have successfully used foundation models to predict CVD-related outcomes, the learned representations in the Study II did not improve prediction of elevated NT-proBNP beyond established clinical predictors and were associated with increased model complexity. The optimism-corrected performance estimates indicate that the more complex models were susceptible to overfitting, reinforcing the conclusion that the learned representations did not provide robust improvements beyond established clinical predictors. This does not necessarily indicate that foundation model-derived representations are unsuitable for cardiovascular prediction. Rather, their performance may depend on the downstream modeling strategy. In Study II, the embeddings were incorporated as fixed predictors in logistic regression models, whereas Lu et al.12 fine-tuned (Figure 1.3, Transfer 1) the CGMformer and achieved an AUROC above 0.70 for predicting macrovascular complications. Similarly, Lutsker et al.13 demonstrated that a GLUFormer-derived risk score outperformed HbA1c in stratifying individuals according to cardiovascular-related mortality risk. However, the limited sample size and the lack of datasets containing both CGM data and cardiovascular outcomes precluded the use of more complex downstream models and task-specific fine-tuning as performed in Lu et al.12 and Lutsker et al.13 Furthermore, reducing the embeddings to UMAP dimensions in Study II may have resulted in the loss of predictive information, which could partly explain the limited performance of the prediction models. The different findings may also reflect differences in the predicted outcomes. Whereas previous studies predicted clinical cardiovascular events or mortality, Study II focused on elevated NT-proBNP, a biomarker that is affected by cardiac dysfunction in addition to non-cardiac factors such as age and renal function.16,17 Consequently, glucose-derived embeddings may have a weaker relationship with NT-proBNP than with clinical cardiovascular outcomes although this remains speculative. Together, these findings suggest that the successful application of foundation model representations to complex clinical outcomes may depend on the downstream modelling strategy and subsequently having access to substantially larger datasets than what was available in Study II.

5.3.1 Learned representations as predictors

While learned representations did not translate into improved prediction of elevated NT-proBNP, TIR and MAGE as standalone predictors did. In Study II, when adding TIR and MAGE to the clinical prediction model resulted in a higher optimism-corrected AUROC compared with the model based on clinical variables alone. However, the performance of these models was also considered relative to a simpler benchmark model based on age alone. The simpler baseline model showed substantial predictive ability, consistent with the well-established association between increasing age and NT-proBNP concentrations in both healthy populations and individuals with T2D.18 Together, these findings suggest that although readily available clinical variables capture much of the information relevant for predicting elevated NT-proBNP, CGM-data may still provide information that could contribute to future cardiovascular risk assessment.

5.3.2 CGM-derived metrics and CVD

Traditional CGM-derived metrics remain highly relevant for clinical prediction. Metrics such as TIR quantify well-established aspects of glycemic control, are readily understood by clinicians, and are already incorporated into routine diabetes management. Their simplicity and established clinical interpretation make them useful reference measures when assessing whether more complex learned representations provide additional predictive information. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated prognostic value for MAGE in individuals with acute coronary syndrome19,20, and greater glucose variability has been associated with higher NT-proBNP concentrations in this population20. Participants with elevated NT-proBNP in the CANCAN cohort exhibited characteristics consistent with more advanced disease but also had lower BMI and more favorable glycemic profiles. Although these findings may appear counterintuitive, lower BMI has previously been associated with poorer outcomes in patients with established heart failure, while obesity is associated with lower circulating NT-proBNP concentrations21. Overall, these findings further illustrates the complex relationship between glycemic control, body composition, and cardiovascular.

5.4 Study III: Fairness of CGM-based Machine Learning Models

While Studies I and II focused on whether learned representations and traditional CGM-derived metrics improved cardiovascular risk prediction, the final aim of the dissertation considered another important aspect of clinical prediction models: whether their performance is equitable across population subgroups.

Study III demonstrated that the racial composition of the training data influenced glucose-forecasting performance across racial groups. Although the observed differences were statistically significant, their magnitude was small and unlikely to be clinically meaningful. These differences were no longer evident after transfer learning, suggesting that adapting models to participant-specific data may reduce performance disparities associated with imbalanced training data. Overall, although training-data composition had only a limited effect on glucose prediction performance in this study, its potential influence should still be assessed when developing and evaluating machine-learning models for clinical use.

Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of transfer learning to improve model performance across various healthcare applications. In multi-ethnic cancer-omics datasets, transfer learning reduced performance disparities, suggesting that it may help mitigate some consequences of imbalanced training data.22 Benefits have also been reported in personalized glucose forecasting, where transfer learning improved performance across several prediction tasks.23,24 Overall, the present findings are largely consistent with the existing literature, as the transfer learned models achieved the best overall performance while also reducing the performance differences observed between racial groups.

5.5 Methodological Considerations

The following section discusses methodological considerations related to the studies included in this dissertation. Particular attention is given to the use of previously collected datasets, the processing and representation of CGM data, model evaluation, dimensionality reduction, and fairness assessment. These considerations provide important context for interpreting the findings and for understanding the strengths and limitations of the methods applied throughout the dissertation.

5.5.1 Study I: The Scoping Review

Study I was a comprehensive mapping of the literature and to the best of my knowledge, identified more relevant studies than any previous review on the topic together with differentiation between association and prediction studies.1,25 The study found methodological heterogeneity, which limited the feasibility of synthesizing effect estimates consistently across studies. The findings were therefore presented descriptively by mapping the number of studies investigating different CGM-derived metrics and cardiovascular outcomes. Although this approach provides an overview of the scope and distribution of the existing evidence, it does not allow definitive conclusions to be drawn about the strength or consistency of associations between specific CGM-derived metrics and cardiovascular outcomes. Furthermore, the predominance of cross-sectional studies found in the scoping review limited the clinical interpretation of the evidence identified. Without temporal separation between CGM exposure and cardiovascular outcomes, it is unclear whether the observed glucose patterns preceded cardiovascular disease or resulted from the disease itself, associated behavioural changes, or intensified treatment. Reverse causality could therefore either produce apparent associations that emerged only after the development of CVD or reduce measurable pre-existing associations through subsequent treatment and behavioural changes. Lastly, only searching two databases might have led to missing relevant studies in other databases, however MEDLINE and Embase were deemed the most relevant biomedical databases and a broad search strategy was prioritized over searching other databases due to the mixed terminology in both CGM-derived metrics and cardiovascular outcomes.

5.5.2 Study II: The CGMformer and Cosine Distances

By externally evaluating CGMformer, developed by Lu et al.,12 Study II contributes to the validation of existing models, as recommended by Efthimiou et al.26 and provides evidence regarding the foundation model’s generalizability. For the evaluation of the foundation model, by combining comparisons with established CGM-derived metrics, cosine-distance analyzes, and visualizations, the study assessed both whether the representations captured clinically interpretable glycemic patterns and participant-specific glycemic characteristics. This provided greater insight into the content and potential utility of the learned representations. A limitation of the cosine-distance analyzes was the complex dependence structure of the observations. Participants contributed multiple days of CGM data and could therefore be represented in multiple distance comparisons. Consequently, the observations were not independent, making a conventional Mann–Whitney U test inappropriate. Statistical significance would also be difficult to interpret because treating all pairwise distances as independent would overstate the effective sample size and could produce misleadingly small \(p\)-values. In addition, the very large number of participant pairs would make even small differences statistically significant, further limiting the interpretability of conventional \(p\)-values. The cosine-distance results were therefore evaluated descriptively and visually, enabling exploration of potential group differences but not formal statistical inference.

5.5.3 Study II: Prediction of NT-proBNP

The use of bootstrapping with optimism correction allowed for robust internal validation, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting and overestimating the results which are important factors to take into consideration when working with limited real-world data. The relatively small sample size restricted the analyzes because the available data was deemed insufficient for task-specific fine-tuning of CGMformer, and the embeddings were instead incorporated as fixed predictors in comparatively simple downstream models. Reducing the embeddings to UMAP dimensions made the number of predictors manageable relative to the sample size but may have discarded information relevant to NT-proBNP. Consequently, the available data did not permit evaluation of the embeddings under task-specific fine-tuning, and their potential prognostic value therefore remains undetermined. Furthermore, dichotomizing NT-proBNP at 125 pg/mL may be an imperfect replacement for cardiovascular risk and heart failure, limiting the extent to which the analyzis could assess the relationship between CGM-derived representations and clinical cardiovascular outcomes. A more definitive evaluation would require substantially larger longitudinal datasets containing temporally aligned CGM measurements, relevant clinical covariates, and subsequent cardiovascular outcomes. However, such datasets remain rare, therefore limiting opportunities for task-specific fine-tuning or the development of more complex prediction models. Access to these data would enable assessment of whether the embeddings provide prognostic information beyond established cardiovascular risk factors and traditional CGM-derived metrics.

5.5.4 Study III: Fairness in Clinical Prediction Models

A key strength of Study III is its systematic evaluation of how the racial composition of training data influences predictive performance across groups. By examining multiple training strategies and varying group representation in the training data, the study provides a framework that could in addition be applied to fairness evaluations involving other populations, outcomes, and data modalities. The use of openly available data, together with publicly shared analytical code, further enhances the transparency and reproducibility of the study and facilitates extension of the approach in future research. However, the study included only non-Hispanic White and Black participants from the United States. Future studies should evaluate potential disparities across additional racial and ethnic groups and in other geographical settings. Fairness evaluations could also consider other demographic characteristics, such as age, given that glycemic control varies across the lifespan.27 Although algorithmic fairness is most commonly evaluated for binary classification using evaluation metrics such as demographic parity and equalized odds,28 comparable approaches for prediction of continuous outcomes remain less developed. Study III therefore provides a novel framework for evaluating disparities in glucose forecasting. However, the analyzis was computationally intensive even though only a shallow neural network was evaluated, because models were repeatedly trained across multiple racial compositions and training strategies for each participant. The computational requirements may therefore limit the feasibility of applying this framework to more complex models without access to considerable computing resources. The findings may also depend on the selected prediction horizon. Although a 60-minute horizon was chosen to allow time for potential preventive action, shorter forecasting horizons generally yield greater predictive accuracy. Future studies should determine whether the observed effects of racial composition and transfer learning are consistent across prediction horizons. In addition, the framework was evaluated using only a single US dataset and was not replicated in an independent cohort. Because the forecasting models were trained separately for each participant, external validation of the fitted models themselves was not directly applicable.
